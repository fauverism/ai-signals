Methodology

Last Week in AI Design is a weekly edition, published every Monday, about AI for UI, UX and visual design: agent skills, UI generation, design tools, and how designers are actually working with AI. Scripts collect and count; an AI model, Claude, judges and writes. This page explains both halves.

What we collect

Every Monday a script reads a published list of feeds and APIs: design publications and UX researchers, design-tool makers, AI labs and tech press, independent writers and community forums. It keeps stories from the last seven days that sit where AI meets UI, UX or visual design (a design story has to mention AI, and an AI story has to be about interfaces, design or designers), removes duplicates, and groups stories about the same event.

What we store and show

For each link: the title, the URL, the source, the author, the publication date, and a short summary written by us in our own words. We never store or show article text, never quote more than 15 words, and never host the original. Every link goes to the original publisher.

How stories are scored

Claude scores each story from 0 to 10 on four measures, using a written rubric, from the title, source and signals. It does not read the full article. The total is computed by code, not by the model. The About page explains each measure, how much it counts, and works through an example.

How stories are featured

  • Lead story: the highest total with credibility of at least 7 that has not been featured before.
  • Top 5: the next five, at most two about the same organization.
  • New innovations: up to six more with novelty of at least 8.
  • Trending now: up to six clusters of related stories, by how far they exceed the topic's recent average.

We skip listicles, stock-price stories, funding news with no product or technical substance, old stories resurfacing without new facts, and anything that is not about UI, UX or visual design.

Limits

Scores are editorial judgments by an AI model and can be wrong. Because the model sees titles and metadata rather than full articles, summaries describe what a story is, not every claim in it. Follow the link for the real thing.

How the scores work

Every story in Last Week in AI Design gets four scores from 0 to 10, and one total that decides where it appears. The scores are judgments, not measurements, so here is exactly what each one asks and how they add up. If you disagree with a ranking, you can see why it landed where it did.

The four scores

Importance

How many designers and design teams this changes things for, and by how much. A story that changes how every product designer works scores higher than one that matters to a handful of specialists.

2
A niche paper, a small plugin's minor update, or a feature for a small group of users.
5
A widely used design tool ships an AI feature its users will adopt, or a solid study of how designers use AI.
9
A release that changes how most product teams design and build interfaces, such as a major tool generating production-ready UI from a design system.

Trend

Is it picking up steam right now? This one mostly follows the numbers: how many independent sources are covering the story, how far the topic is above its usual size, and how strongly people are reacting on forums and code sites. The scorer may move it up or down by at most 2 points, and has to give a reason.

2
One or two sources, no history of the topic, little reaction.
5
Three sources and a lively discussion.
9
Five or more independent sources, a topic far above its usual size, and strong reaction.

Novelty

Is it actually new? A new capability, method or first-of-its-kind release scores high. A recap, a roundup, a small update, or an opinion about last month's news scores low. A story we already featured is judged only on what has changed since.

2
A weekly roundup, or a take on an old release.
5
A new version of something existing, with measurable gains.
9
Something that wasn't possible before, or a new method with credible results that beat the best so far.

Credibility

Can you trust it? Stories from the source itself, with named authors and something you can check, score high. Rumor, unsourced claims and rewrites of someone else's announcement score low. The kind of outlet is a starting point, not the answer: a preprint is not peer reviewed just because it's a paper.

2
An anonymous rumor, or a rewrite of someone else's announcement with no link to the original.
5
A reputable outlet reporting secondhand, or a paper without code or data.
9
The lab's own announcement, or a paper with code and data from named authors.

How they add up

The four scores are blended into one total. Some count more than others:

ScoreWeightShare of the total
Importance35%
Trend25%
Novelty25%
Credibility15%

Here is a worked example. A story scores Importance 8, Trend 6, Novelty 7 and Credibility 9:

0.35 × 8 = 2.80
0.25 × 6 = 1.50
0.25 × 7 = 1.75
0.15 × 9 = 1.35
total    = 7.4  (rounded to one decimal)

One safeguard: if Credibility is below 4, the total can never be higher than 5, however exciting the story sounds. A great claim you can't trust doesn't get to top the page.

On each card, the word beside a number is a plain-language label: 0–2 Low, 3–4 Modest, 5–6 Solid, 7–8 Strong, 9–10 Exceptional.

Who does the scoring

An AI model, Claude, scores each story against a written rubric. It sees the title, source, author, date and the community signals, but not the full article, so it judges what a story is, not every claim inside it. The totals and the final order are then worked out by code from those four numbers, not by the model. The same scores always produce the same ranking.

Scores are opinions and can be wrong. Treat them as a guide to where to look first, and follow the link to the original for the real thing.
